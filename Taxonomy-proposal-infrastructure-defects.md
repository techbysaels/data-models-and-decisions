# Taxonomy proposal for defects in transportation infrastructure

As the person responsible for the reliability and long-term performance of our built infrastructure, I propose that our organization adopt an ML-enabled monitoring and sensing process for the detection of defects in transportation infrastructure. As the board knows, our current inspection model depends on teams of professionals deployed to physically examine the infrastructure and record visible deteriorations. This requires considerable time and specialized resources that could be doing more important tasks like analysing and developing repair plans. To address this, the proposed approach would use images and three-dimensional measurements acquired through drones, laser sensors, and vehicle-mounted cameras as inputs to computer vision models capable of locating and classifying defects like pavement cracks or potholes.

In my opinion, ML-enabled monitoring and sensing is the strongest approach for this application because detection and classification of infrastructure defects are essentially a pattern recognition problem. The model can be trained using labelled examples from our historical data on normal and damaged surfaces. When an image is analysed, the model will identify features associated with deterioration that, depending on the implementation, will produce our desired outputs: type of defect, location, and outline of the affected surface. These results could then be integrated into the inspection records, creating a structured historical database of infrastructure condition. Additionally, this architecture would allow us to inspect large areas in record time using inspection vehicles and drones. Inspection vehicles will collect data while traveling through road networks, while drones will be able to reach areas difficult to access for the professional teams. Another advantage would be consistency, because human inspections inevitably involve differences in experience, fatigue, judgment, and interpretation, while a validated ML model will apply the same learned parameters to every processed image, creating also digital records that can be compared over time to detect if a crack is stable or is increasing in severity.

I would not recommend rule-based automation because this system works effectively when inputs and outputs are highly structured and clearly follow a defined rule. This is not the case when images of defects might vary in size, texture, lighting, camera angles, shadows, and surrounding conditions. These variations make it difficult to define explicit rules because they would be fragile and not consistent over time. Also, an ML-enabled automation that acts on detected defects without expert validation would not be appropriate because structural decisions carry significant liability and safety risks when the model produces false positives or negatives. It is worth noting that personalization does not apply to the use case because inspection is not a task that could be individualized.

Finally, I would recommend that our engineers and inspectors participate in the process by reviewing flagged cases, confirming important defects, and determining repair priorities. This solution intends to supplement and automate a portion of the manual inspection rather than eliminate professional involvement. Considering that, we could shift our specialists to tasks where their expertise provides the greatest value for our business: confirming critical defects, assessing structural implications, prioritising interventions, and developing effective maintenance plans.
